Carbon Capture: Removing CO2 from the Atmosphere

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies are increasingly recognized as essential components of the global strategy to combat climate change. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has emphasized that achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 will likely require both drastic emissions reductions and active removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Point-source carbon capture involves capturing CO2 emissions directly from industrial facilities such as power plants, cement factories, and steel mills before they enter the atmosphere. The technology typically uses chemical solvents, most commonly amine-based solutions, to absorb CO2 from flue gases. The captured CO2 is then compressed and transported, usually by pipeline, to underground geological formations for permanent storage. The Sleipner project in Norway, operational since 1996, was the world's first commercial-scale CCS operation, storing approximately one million tonnes of CO2 annually beneath the North Sea.

Direct air capture (DAC) represents a more ambitious approach, extracting CO2 directly from ambient air where concentrations are roughly 420 parts per million, far more dilute than industrial flue gases. Two leading DAC companies have emerged: Climeworks, based in Switzerland, and Carbon Engineering, a Canadian company acquired by Occidental Petroleum. Climeworks operates the Orca plant in Iceland, which captures approximately 4,000 tonnes of CO2 per year, and is building the larger Mammoth facility with ten times the capacity. The captured CO2 is mineralized by injection into basalt rock formations through a process developed by Carbfix.

The US Department of Energy has launched the Regional Direct Air Capture Hubs program, allocating 3.5 billion dollars to develop four large-scale DAC facilities across the country. The goal is to demonstrate the technology at the scale of one million tonnes of CO2 removal per year per hub. The Inflation Reduction Act enhanced the 45Q tax credit for carbon capture, providing up to 180 dollars per tonne for DAC and 85 dollars per tonne for point-source capture with geological storage.

Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) extends the concept by finding productive uses for captured CO2 rather than simply storing it. Applications include enhanced oil recovery (EOR), where CO2 is injected into depleted oil fields to extract additional petroleum, producing building materials such as carbon-cured concrete through companies like CarbonCure Technologies, and synthesizing sustainable aviation fuels. While utilization pathways can offset capture costs, critics argue that using captured CO2 for enhanced oil recovery undermines climate goals.

Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) combines biomass energy production with CCS technology to achieve negative emissions. In this process, biomass such as agricultural residues or purpose-grown energy crops absorbs CO2 from the atmosphere during growth. When the biomass is burned for energy and the resulting emissions are captured and stored, the net effect is removal of CO2 from the atmosphere. The Drax power station in the United Kingdom is piloting BECCS technology.

Ocean-based carbon removal is an emerging frontier. Approaches include ocean alkalinity enhancement, which involves adding alkaline minerals to seawater to increase its capacity to absorb CO2, and cultivating seaweed or kelp forests that sequester carbon through photosynthesis. Companies like Running Tide and Planetary Technologies are developing commercial ocean carbon removal methods.

The cost of carbon capture remains a significant barrier. Point-source capture costs range from 15 to 120 dollars per tonne of CO2, depending on the concentration of CO2 in the source gas. DAC costs are currently between 400 and 1,000 dollars per tonne, though projections suggest costs could fall to 100 to 200 dollars per tonne with scale and technological improvements. Achieving gigaton-scale carbon removal will require continued innovation, supportive policy, and substantial investment from both public and private sectors.
